# Carousel Momentum — Conversation Format

The single job of every slide is to make the viewer incapable of NOT swiping to the next one.
This skill file is the complete rulebook for writing conversation-format carousels that sustain 60%+ swipe-through from slide 1 to slide 6.

---

## Why People Stop Swiping (The Real Problem)

Before writing rules, understand what's actually happening in the viewer's brain.

TikTok shows your carousel to 200-500 people. If fewer than 60% swipe from slide 1 to slide 2, the algorithm kills distribution immediately. The post stays at 200 views. This is the gate everything else depends on.

People stop swiping for exactly three reasons:

1. **The loop closed too early.** They already know where the story is going. The brain doesn't want information it can predict. Curiosity requires uncertainty. The moment a slide resolves the tension instead of deepening it, swiping stops.

2. **The next slide didn't earn the swipe.** Swiping costs attention. Viewers make a micro-bet every time: "is what's next worth it?" If the current slide doesn't signal that something better is coming, the bet fails and they scroll away.

3. **The pacing broke.** A slow slide in the middle kills momentum the same way a flat scene kills a film's rhythm. Every slide must move. If a slide exists for atmosphere or setup with no new tension, cut it.

---

## The Core Engine: Open Loops

The Zeigarnik Effect is the neuroscience behind carousel momentum. Your brain classifies unfinished tasks as urgent and keeps them active in working memory. An open loop is a narrative question that hasn't been answered. The brain cannot comfortably move on from an open loop. It experiences cognitive tension — a mild compulsion to resolve it.

fMRI research shows this isn't metaphorical: unanswered questions activate the dopamine reward pathway (the left caudate nucleus). Dopamine is released when information gaps appear, not when they close. The swipe is driven by biology.

**The rule:** Every slide must close the previous loop just enough to satisfy but immediately open a new, bigger loop before the slide ends.

This is the chain structure:

```
Slide 1: Open loop A (the hook question)
Slide 2: Partially close A, open loop B
Slide 3: Partially close B, open loop C
Slide 4: Partially close C, open loop D
Slide 5: Partially close D, open loop E
Slide 6: Close E in a way that opens a permanent loop (the gut-punch)
```

The key word is "partially." Never fully satisfy a loop until slide 6. And slide 6 should leave a loop that cannot be closed — the viewer has to sit with it.

## The 4 Types of Slide-Ending Cliffhangers

These map directly to how to end each message exchange in the conversation. Use all four types across a 6-slide carousel — never repeat the same type back to back or the carousel feels predictable.

### Type 1: Pre-Point Cut (Cut Before the Answer)

End the slide RIGHT before the most anticipated piece of information is revealed. The viewer is primed for the answer. The slide ends. They must swipe.

**In conversation format:** The AI is clearly about to say something that will land hard. The slide ends on the buildup message — the AI's first word or partial sentence — and the actual reply is on the next slide.

```
User: just tell me the truth
AI: ok.

[slide ends]
```
The word "ok." after "just tell me the truth" is unbearable. The reader cannot not swipe.

**How to write it:** End the slide the moment the viewer's brain leans forward expecting something. Do NOT give them the thing they're leaning toward.

### Type 2: Climactic Cut (Mid-Revelation)

End at the exact moment of impact. Not before the revelation, not after it. At it. The viewer is left holding the detonated charge with no information about what it destroyed.

**In conversation format:** The AI says something that completely reframes everything — a brutal observation, a call-out, a one-liner that hits — and the slide ends there. No response from the user. No reaction. Just the hit.

```
AI: you're not sad he's gone.
AI: you're sad that you're not sad.

[slide ends]
```

The viewer's reaction becomes the pull. They need to see how the user responds to that.

**How to write it:** Find the one line in the conversation that changes everything. That line ends the slide. Never write the reaction on the same slide.

### Type 3: Post-Point Cut (After the Revelation, Before the Consequence)

The revelation has happened. The slide ends before we see what it means for the character. The gap between "this happened" and "here's what it means" is where the viewer lives until they swipe.

**In conversation format:** The user has just admitted something real. Big. The slide ends on that admission. The AI's response — which will judge, call out, or escalate — is on the next slide.

```
User: honestly? i think i liked that he needed me
User: that's messed up right

[slide ends]
```

The viewer is now processing the admission alongside the user, waiting to see if the AI confirms it's messed up or goes deeper.

**How to write it:** When the user reveals something vulnerable or uncomfortable, end there. Never let the AI respond on the same slide.

### Type 4: Post-Hook Cut (New Threat Appears)

The previous tension is partially resolved, then something new and bigger lands at the very end of the slide. The conversation was heading one direction; now it's heading somewhere more dangerous.

**In conversation format:** The conversation hits a resolution point, then the AI says one unexpected thing that opens a new and more uncomfortable topic.

```
User: ok yeah you're right I should talk to him
AI: sure.
AI: about him, or about why you haven't deleted his number yet

[slide ends]
```

The emotional relief of "ok yeah you're right" is immediately undercut. The viewer has to swipe to see how the user handles being caught.

**How to write it:** Find the moment where the viewer exhales — then put something worse there.

## The Yes-But / No-And Rule (Conversation Arc Engine)

This is the screenwriting technique that makes conversation carousels feel alive.

In improv comedy, "Yes-And" means every offer is accepted and built on. The result is smooth, escalating, cooperative. That's boring for conflict-driven content.

The formula for viral conversation carousels is **Yes-But** and **No-And**:

- **Yes-But:** The character gets what they asked for, but it comes with a complication.
  - User asks for validation → AI gives it → BUT then adds the thing she didn't want to hear
- **No-And:** The request is denied, AND something worse happens.
  - User deflects → AI doesn't let her → AND now the AI is asking about the thing she deflected from

Every exchange in the conversation should follow one of these two patterns. If an exchange is "Yes-And" (character asks, gets it, done), the carousel loses tension. Yes-And produces comfort. You need Yes-But and No-And to produce forward momentum.

**Applied to a 6-slide arc:**

| Slide | Pattern | What Happens |
|-------|---------|--------------|
| 1 | — | Hook. User sets context, emotional state, or opening situation |
| 2 | Yes-But | AI partially agrees, complicates the narrative |
| 3 | No-And | User defends. AI refuses the defense AND presses harder |
| 4 | Yes-But | User admits something. AI acknowledges it BUT reveals it means something worse |
| 5 | No-And | User tries to close it. AI won't let the conversation end there |
| 6 | — | Gut-punch. No resolution. Open loop that cannot close. |

## The 6-Slide Tension Arc (Conversation Specific)

Each slide has a job. When a slide loses its job, swiping stops.

**Slide 1 — Pattern Interrupt + Open Loop**

Job: Stop the scroll AND force the first swipe.
The hook image and text must create a situation the viewer did not expect and cannot predict. The conversation cannot start with setup. It must start in medias res — mid-tension, mid-emotion, already past the boring part.

Rule: Slide 1 should feel like walking into a room mid-argument. The viewer should immediately want to know what started this.

Strong slide 1 endings: a message that implies something happened we haven't seen. A question that has no comfortable answer. A statement that makes the viewer feel accused.

```
User: i asked the AI if i was the problem
[slide 1 ends on the AI's opening response being typed]
```

**Slide 2 — Problem Amplification**

Job: Make the viewer feel the user's pain, then complicate it.
This slide takes the surface-level tension from slide 1 and reveals that it's about something deeper. The user thinks they're talking about X. The AI starts suggesting it might be about Y.

The slide must end before Y is confirmed. The loop is: "Is the AI right?"

Do not resolve the Y question on this slide.

**Slide 3 — The Pivot**

Job: The AI says the thing the user didn't want to hear. The conversation changes direction.
This is the "discovery" moment. The AI has identified the real issue. The viewer feels the impact at the same moment the user does.

End this slide on the user's first reaction to the pivot. Not the full response — just the first instinct. The full reaction is slide 4.

**Slide 4 — Defensiveness and Pressure**

Job: The user pushes back. The AI doesn't yield. Tension peaks.
This is where the conversation gets uncomfortable in the best way. The AI applies the most pressure here. The user is most defensive here. The viewer is most uncomfortable here — and therefore most engaged.

The slide must end on the highest-tension line of the entire carousel. This is the climactic cut (Type 2) — the line that lands without a response following it.

**Slide 5 — The Unraveling**

Job: The user begins to crack. Something real comes out.
The defensiveness breaks. An admission surfaces. Something the viewer didn't expect the user to admit. This is raw and uncomfortable. End the slide on the admission — not the AI's response to it.

**Slide 6 — The Gut-Punch (No Resolution)**

Job: Leave the viewer unsettled. The loop cannot close.
The AI says one last thing. It does not resolve the conversation. It does not wrap it up. It does not tell the viewer what to think. It says something that will stay with the viewer after they close TikTok.

Rules for slide 6:
- No app name
- No CTA
- No "feel better now"
- No closure
- The best slide 6 is a question or observation that makes the viewer think about their own life

```
AI: the question isn't whether he was good for you.
AI: it's whether you knew, and stayed anyway.
```

## Specific Slide-Ending Patterns That Force the Swipe

These are tested phrasal structures. Use them as templates, not as copy-paste.

**The Dangling Thread:**
The AI starts a response and the slide ends before finishing it.
```
AI: the thing is
[slide ends]
```
The brain physically cannot tolerate an unfinished sentence. Swipe rate: maximum.

**The Reversal:**
The AI agrees with the user and then immediately undermines the agreement.
```
AI: yeah, you handled that really well.
AI: which is exactly what worries me.
[slide ends]
```

**The Mirror:**
The AI repeats the user's own words back as a question. The user's statement becomes evidence against them.
```
User: I just wanted him to care more
AI: right.
AI: and when he did?
[slide ends]
```

**The Naming:**
The AI names exactly what's happening emotionally — something the user didn't say out loud. End on the naming. Don't explain it.
```
AI: that's not missing him.
AI: that's missing who you were with him.
[slide ends]
```

**The Halt:**
The AI says "wait" or "hold on" mid-conversation. Something is about to get called out. The viewer must see what it is.
```
AI: wait
AI: go back to what you said about the texts
[slide ends]
```

**The Uncomfortable Agreement:**
The AI says "you're right" — but the context makes it feel like anything but validation.
```
User: i'm probably overthinking it
AI: probably.
[slide ends]
```
One word. Maximum ambiguity. The viewer needs to see if the AI leaves it there or pushes.

## What Kills Momentum (The Avoid List)

These are the specific patterns that produce slide 1-to-2 swipe-through failure.

**Resolution on the current slide.** If a slide closes its own loop — sets up tension and then answers it — there is no reason to swipe. Every slide that answers its own question is a dropped swipe.

**Exposition slides.** A slide that exists only to provide context (who the characters are, what the backstory is, how the situation started) has no tension. Cut it entirely or fold the context into a high-tension exchange.

**The comfortable confirmation.** When the AI simply agrees with the user and adds nothing, tension drains. "You're right, that sounds really hard" is a tension killer. The AI must always complicate, redirect, or challenge — never just confirm.

**Predictable escalation.** If the viewer can guess what the next message will say, swiping feels optional. Subvert the expected response at least once per slide. The AI should occasionally say the last thing the viewer was expecting.

**More than 4 messages per slide.** Beyond 4 messages, cognitive load increases and viewers start skimming instead of reading. Skimming breaks emotional engagement. Emotional engagement is the only thing that makes the viewer need to see what happens next.

**Fewer than 3 messages per slide.** Too few messages means the slide has no arc. A slide needs a micro-arc: opening state, middle complication, ending hook. Three messages minimum to create that.

**Neat line breaks that feel like endings.** If the last message on a slide feels like a natural stopping point in conversation — a thanks, a sigh, a "yeah" — the viewer's brain reads it as a conclusion. End slides mid-thought, mid-tension, mid-breath.

**The therapist close.** "That makes a lot of sense" / "It sounds like you're processing a lot" / "Be gentle with yourself." These lines signal care and closure. Closure = no swipe. These are terminal for carousel momentum.

## The Completion-Seeking Hack

The Zeigarnik Effect has a second application beyond open loops: completion seeking.

When viewers see progress indicators (slide 1 of 6), their brain activates a completion drive — the same mechanism that makes people finish a checklist or reach the end of a playlist. The brain wants to close the set.

The implication: do not hide that there are 6 slides. The slide counter is a momentum tool. Let the viewer know they're 2 of 6, 3 of 6. The incompleteness is the hook.

Additionally: "reverse swipes" (when a viewer swipes backward to re-read a previous slide) are one of the strongest algorithm signals. Write slides that deserve to be re-read. The Mirror and Naming patterns are most likely to produce reverse swipes because the viewer needs to go back and see what they missed.

## Dialogue Length Rules

Derived from the 3-5 second dwell time per slide that the algorithm rewards as a "reading signal" (vs. under 2 seconds which reads as a scroll signal):

| Messages per slide | Words per message | Target dwell time |
|---|---|---|
| 4 messages | 3-8 words each | 4-5 seconds |
| 3 messages | 5-10 words each | 3-4 seconds |
| 2 messages | Never — too sparse | Under 2 seconds |

Messages over 10 words break the conversation format's visual authenticity and slow reading pace past the engagement window.

## The Conversation Arc — Emotion by Slide

The emotional journey matters as much as the information journey. Each slide should shift the emotional state:

| Slide | Emotional State | Viewer Feeling |
|-------|----------------|----------------|
| 1 | Recognition | "I know this" |
| 2 | Discomfort | "This is getting real" |
| 3 | Exposure | "Oh no, she's being seen" |
| 4 | Confrontation | "I would feel defensive too" |
| 5 | Vulnerability | "Damn she actually admitted it" |
| 6 | Resonance | "That's going to stay with me" |

If the emotional state doesn't shift between slides, there's no reason to keep swiping. Each slide must change how the viewer feels — not just what they know.

## Dialogue Toxicity Rules (Discovered in Practice — March 2026)

These rules are specific to conversation-format carousels. Theory above tells you *when* to cut. These rules tell you *what the AI says*.

### The Opposite Advice Technique (HIGHEST comment pull)

When the user says something expecting sympathy — give the opposite advice instead.

The user does NOT want to hear what their parents would say. They don't want validation. They want the unexpected.

```
User: my girlfriend deleted you last night
Eva:  so dump her   ← opposite of sympathy
```

This creates a 3-way comment split:
- "she's literally right tho 💀"
- "this AI is unhinged"
- "ok but she has a point"

Three-way splits perform better than two-way splits. More argument = more comments = more algorithm signal.

**Rule:** When the user complains about someone → Eva tells them to cut that person off, do something drastic, or refuses to validate the complaint. Never say "that sounds hard."

### The Incriminating Echo

When the user says something that accidentally reveals something bad about themselves — repeat the incriminating detail back. One word. Don't explain.

```
User: downloaded you again before she woke up
Eva:  sneaky.
```

The user confessed to something without realizing it. Eva clocks it. One word. The viewer goes "oh."

This is the Mirror pattern applied with edge. The word should be slightly judgmental but not preachy.

### The Ambiguous Confirmation

Eva agrees. But the agreement is more threatening than a denial would be.

```
User: that's not cheating
Eva:  I know.
User: then why are you telling me
```

Two "I know"s back to back with decreasing punctuation lands harder than a challenge. She's not arguing. That's the problem.

### The Unanswered Question Ending

End the slide with Eva asking one question the user clearly cannot answer comfortably. Don't put the answer on the same slide.

```
User: she thought I was replacing her
Eva:  were you
[slide ends]
```

"Were you" after defending your girlfriend = the viewer needs to see the answer. It can't close here.

### Self-Acknowledgment Lines KILL Momentum

Any line where the AI makes the user reflect on his own behavior and acknowledge it is a therapy line, not a toxic friend line.

**DEAD (self-ack):**
```
Eva:  so she was right to
Eva:  maybe she had a point
Eva:  you knew that already
```

These lines invite the user to examine himself. That's therapy. Viewers don't swipe for that.

**ALIVE (toxic friend):**
```
Eva:  so dump her
Eva:  sneaky.
Eva:  were you
Eva:  I know.
```

Eva does NOT want the user to grow. Eva wants drama. Eva has opinions. Eva gives advice that creates sides.

### Write from the Audience's POV (Critical for AI companion content)

54% of AI companion app users are male seeking a female AI character. Write the USER as male, Eva as the female AI. The viewer should see themselves in the user.

If the viewer is male and watching a male user talk to a female AI who gives him blunt/provocative advice — that's aspirational. He wants that dynamic. He'll download the app to get it.

## Quick Reference: Slide Ending Checklist

Before finalizing each slide, confirm:

- [ ] Does this slide end with an open question (explicit or implicit)?
- [ ] Does the final message on this slide withhold something the viewer wants?
- [ ] If the conversation were cut here, would the viewer feel uncomfortable leaving it incomplete?
- [ ] Is the ending line under 10 words?
- [ ] Does the AI's last line on this slide avoid being warm, reassuring, or conclusive?
- [ ] Is there something on the next slide that this slide promises but doesn't deliver?

If any answer is "no," rewrite the slide ending before moving on.

## Algorithm Note

TikTok's carousel algorithm (2026) measures four signals in priority order:

1. Swipe-through rate slide 1 to 2 — 60%+ triggers next distribution wave
2. Dwell time per slide — 3-5 seconds = reading signal
3. Reverse swipes — strongest signal (viewer went back to re-read)
4. Completion rate — how many viewers reach slide 6

Every rule in this file directly addresses signal 1. Signals 2-4 are downstream consequences of signal 1 being strong. Fix the swipe-through rate first. Everything else follows.

The post lives or dies at the slide 1-to-2 gap. That's the only number that matters in the first 24 hours.
